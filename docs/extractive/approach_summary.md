# Extractive Royalty Carve-Out: Approach Summary

> ⚠️ **SUPERSEDED / LEGACY (kept for reference).** This document describes the
> earlier **carve-out-then-UT** approach: carve a minimum royalty out of CbCR
> profit, then run UT once on the remainder (`3_resource_contribution.py` →
> `4_carveout.py` → `5b_carveout_then_ut.py`, now moved to `src/archive/`). It
> has been **replaced by the dataset-adjustment scheme** — build several
> resource-adjusted copies of the CbCR dataset and run the *same* UT on each
> (`4_correcting_cbcr_for_resource_payments.py` → `5_estimate_profit_shifting.py`).
> The live methodology is documented in
> [`resource_payment_correction.md`](resource_payment_correction.md) and
> [`../scenarios_methodology.md`](../scenarios_methodology.md). The carve-out
> scripts are retained as a backup reference only and are **not** part of the
> orchestrated pipeline (`src/_run_pipeline.py`).

Before applying formulary apportionment to reallocate MNE profits, we carve out a minimum royalty that source countries retain on the value extracted from their natural resources. Per source country and commodity category (oil/gas, coal, minerals), the layered best estimate of resource rent is built from a gross-revenue base × a rent fraction. The gross-revenue base for EITI-reporting countries uses a **3-way blend** (preferred → fallback): the EITI-disclosed sales value in USD, else the EITI-disclosed value in local currency converted at the World Bank exchange rate, else the reconstructed `volume × reference price`. Where EITI has no data, BGS or EIA production volumes (× reference price) feed in, and where none of those exist, the World Bank `Resource Rents` indicator is the last-priority fallback. Rent fractions are country-specific medians reverse-engineered from the WB indicator on overlap years, calibrated against the same revenue series. The EITI cleaning step (`2_7_eiti_to_clean_panel.py`) also normalises non-English / mojibake unit strings, reconstructs gas-by-mass and other alternative reporting, fixes clean power-of-10 volume mislabels by cross-checking against the disclosed value, drops the unidentifiable "Other ores" (HS 2617) catch-all, and recovers values that leaked into the volume column under a currency-code "unit". Where captured government revenue exceeds the rent estimate (a producer the cost-curve under-prices), the rent is scaled up to match capture (`rent_scaled_to_capture = True`).

We then compute three variants of the variable royalty in parallel — Categories 1, 2, and 3 from IISD/IGF (Lassourd & Manley 2022). **Category 1** sets the rate as a function of the commodity's reference price for the year (1% at the historical low, 10% at the historical high) and applies it to gross extractive revenue (volume × price). For minerals — a heterogeneous basket — the rate is computed per HS-code commodity (each mineral keyed to its own benchmark price) and blended by each country's revenue-weighted production mix; the per-mineral breakdown is written to `rents_per_mineral_yearly.csv` and `cbcr_main_carveout_per_mineral.csv`. **Category 2** keeps the same gross-revenue base but moves the rate with the partner-year operating margin (1%–10% across margins of 0–60%). **Category 3** is closer to a variable profit tax: the rate moves with operating margin (1%–12%) and is applied directly to resource rent as the operating-profit proxy. All three variants are restricted to the cross-border IOC pool — the share of rent the producing state has *not* already captured through any fiscal channel, i.e. `1 − state_share` where `state_share = captured_total / rent_total`, capped at 1. `captured_total` is deliberately instrument-agnostic: royalties, excise, levies, bonuses/fees, SOE dividends / state PSA equity, **and** corporate income tax on resource profit all count, because a state that captures its rent mostly via CIT (many regimes barely use royalties) should be protected exactly like one that uses royalties — otherwise the carve-out would re-expose CIT-reliant producers to having their rent formulary-apportioned away by the employee/payroll factors. The over-counting risk — that the EITI "CIT" line is sometimes just ordinary CIT on *non-rent* corporate profit, which UT should redistribute — is handled by the rent cap, not by excluding CIT: ordinary CIT on non-rent profit pushes `captured_total` above the rent estimate, where it gets clipped (and the rent-scaling floor in script 3 §3d deliberately does **not** scale the rent up to absorb CIT, so the clip actually bites). What survives the clip is rent-sized capture, which the producing country legitimately retains; the residual profit (including the ordinary-CIT base) flows through the IOC → UT machinery. The same treatment naturally handles multinational SOEs (Aramco, Equinor, Petrobras, Rosneft/Gazprom, …): the SOE keeps its rent via the royalty/equity/CIT terms, while whatever residual profit it books — already inside CbCR — still goes through UT.

To deduct these royalties from the CbCR profit pool, we need to know which MNE headquarter countries are responsible for what share of global extraction. We compiled revenue-weighted shares from the ~100 largest extractive companies (IOCs and NOCs), classified by HQ country and commodity type. These shares differ substantially by commodity — for instance, China and the US dominate oil/gas, while Australia, UK, and Canada lead in minerals. Only HQ countries that report CbCR data are subject to deductions; non-reporters drop out automatically, which means the carve-out only applies to the profit pool we can actually observe and reallocate. By the same token, the government resource revenue reported in EITI/GRD includes payments from non-multinational, purely domestic extractors (NOCs that don't file CbCR, small private firms) that have no CbCR profit counterpart — that revenue is scaled out of the carve-out base and the comparison (it stays with the host country and is simply unaffected by the reform). In practice this puts a large fraction of the world's reported resource government revenue — the Gulf NOC states above all — out of scope.

The amount each producing country retains under "carve-out + UT" is just a country-year quantity: `resource_take = max( current_resource_revenue , modelled_minimum_royalty )` — the larger of what it collects today and our modelled minimum royalty. No row allocation is needed for that figure; for almost every country it equals the current take (they already collect more than the modelled minimum), and only genuine undertaxers get bumped up. (There is a notional "max = 100% of rent" ceiling, but it is calibrated to never bind below the current take — in the data it would clip ≈ $0/yr, the rent-scaling floor already having lifted the rent estimate to match capture — so effectively there is no cap.) The *per-(parent, partner) cell* machinery does only one thing: work out *which* CbCR cells the carve-out comes out of — i.e. which parents' profit pools shrink before the UT step. Of the retained claim, the part already expensed in CbCR profit-before-tax (royalties / excise / levies) is already out of the pool; the remainder — the royalty top-up above what the state already takes, plus retained resource CIT, plus state equity / other still in pre-tax profit — is carved out of CbCR profit-before-tax cell by cell (clipped to the positive profit booked in the cell). There is no "add back then re-subtract": the netting is built in — if the state already meets the minimum, only the CIT/equity portion comes out and the expensed royalties stay where they are. So no country gets less on its resources than it currently does; a producing country can still come out behind under "carve-out + UT" only on the *CIT* side — for the same reason any country does under plain UT, because formulary apportionment assigns it less profit than it currently books and taxes.

After deducting the carve-out from CbCR profits by parent jurisdiction, the remaining (reduced) profit pool enters the existing formulary apportionment and misalignment analysis (`5b_carveout_then_ut.py` runs the two-step "carve-out then UT" and writes the extractive-accounting tables). This two-track approach ensures that resource-rich countries' claims on extraction value are respected as a prior right — before any unitary taxation redistributes the remainder — protecting both IOC host countries and NOC/SOE home countries from losing taxing rights over their natural resources.
